Chapter Twelve

# Scapegoats Are Structurally Selected

Collapse does not distribute evenly.

It concentrates.

Within systems that externalize regulation, certain targets absorb disproportionate load. These targets are commonly described as unlucky, sensitive, or uniquely involved. Structurally, they are selected.

Scapegoating is not primarily a narrative act. It is a regulatory one.

Selection occurs where absorption is cheapest. The system routes toward, implicitly, where regulation can be completed with minimal return cost. Visibility, accessibility, and conscience lower the price of displacement. The scapegoat is not chosen because of who they are, but because of what they can absorb.

Several conditions increase selection probability.

First, availability. Systems that remain present, responsive, or invested provide a reliable surface for externalization. Withdrawal increases cost. Presence lowers it.

Second, asymmetry. Where leverage favors the originating system through role, authority, social standing, or informational control, regulation can move outward without rebound. The scapegoat cannot return cost without penalty.

Third, coherence. Systems that maintain internal order will attempt to correct strain rather than deflect it. They internalize responsibility. This internalization completes regulation efficiently for the originating system.

Fourth, visibility. Systems that can be observed or evaluated provide feedback without demanding it. Their reactions supply information. Their distress confirms completion. Regulation finds closure.

These conditions are structural, not moral. They explain why scapegoats are often competent, conscientious, or well-intentioned. These traits reduce friction. They do not protect.

Once selected, the scapegoat’s role stabilizes. Regulation is routed predictably. Deviations are corrected. If the scapegoat resists, the system reallocates pressure until compliance or withdrawal occurs. Either outcome completes regulation.

Removal does not resolve the problem.

When a scapegoat exits, regulation does not disappear. It seeks a new surface. Selection criteria remain unchanged. A replacement emerges. This is why removing individuals rarely corrects systemic dysfunction. The pathway persists.

Scapegoats often attempt to explain what is happening. These explanations fail to correct the structure because explanation does not alter asymmetry. In some cases, explanation increases selection by confirming visibility and engagement.

This is why scapegoating is often misinterpreted as interpersonal conflict.

From the inside, the experience feels personal. From the outside, it appears contingent. Structurally, it is neither. It is the predictable outcome of externalized regulation interacting with asymmetry and access.

Scapegoats also experience identity distortion.

As regulatory load accumulates, the receiving system begins to reorganize around the pressure. Self-concept shifts. Confidence erodes. Function narrows. These changes are then used to justify further selection. The system reads consequence as cause.

This recursive loop deepens isolation.

Observers may attribute scapegoating to prejudice, malice, or misunderstanding. These attributions capture surface features but miss the mechanism. Even when such factors are present, they are not required. Selection operates without them.

Scapegoating persists because it works.

Regulation completes. Stability is preserved. The cost is paid elsewhere. Until the structure changes, the process repeats. Appeals to fairness, empathy, or awareness do not alter selection criteria. They do not raise the cost of displacement.

Only structural change interrupts selection.

Absent that change, scapegoats are not punished for deviation. They are used for regulation. The distinction matters.
