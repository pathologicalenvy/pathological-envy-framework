# REORGANIZATION AFTER SOLVABILITY IS EXCEEDED

Once internal regulation reaches its limit, correction is no longer possible. The system cannot update, integrate, or absorb discrepancy without destabilizing its own coherence. At this boundary, continued exposure does not produce learning or adjustment. It produces a forced transition in form.

This chapter does not introduce new mechanisms. It identifies what becomes structurally inevitable once existing mechanisms fail.

### From Correction to Reorganization

Up to this point, instability has been described in terms of regulatory strain: escalating effort, narrowing tolerance, increased reliance on asymmetry, and progressive externalization. These processes operate so long as internal correction remains feasible. When correction fails because discrepancy exceeds capacity, or because scaling cannot occur, or because ambiguity cannot be tolerated, the system must reorganize or collapse.

Reorganization is not deterioration. It is a structural solution to an unsolvable constraint. The system preserves continuity by altering the way reality is bound, rather than by altering itself to meet reality. Stability is restored, but the cost is borne elsewhere: in coherence, in reciprocity, or in shared reference.

This transition marks the final internal state available to the system before consequences propagate outward.

### Psychosis as Structural Reorganization

Psychosis is treated here as a form of reorganization, not as a diagnosis, symptom cluster, or pathology. It refers to any configuration in which shared-reality binding is modified or suspended in order to restore internal stability when correction is no longer possible.

Different psychoses exist because different failures require different reorganizations. There is no singular psychotic state. There is an outcome space.

What unites these outcomes is not content, intensity, or duration, but function: each restores stability under constraint by replacing correction with reorganization.

### Psychosis Hook Points

Reorganization does not occur arbitrarily. It becomes viable only at specific structural boundaries: points at which ordinary regulation can no longer succeed.

These boundaries include:
**Narrative lock boundaries**, where ambiguity threatens coherence and certainty is imposed to eliminate error.
**Mortification boundaries**, where exposure exceeds containability and internal solvability is irreversibly breached.
**Convergence failure boundaries**, where repeated stabilization attempts fail to consolidate and no shared model can be maintained.
**Object reconstitution boundaries**, where coherence is preserved by reconstructing the object rather than integrating contradiction.

At each of these points, reorganization becomes less costly than continued correction.

### Governing Variables

At a psychosis hook point, the form reorganization takes is not interpretive or contingent. It is determined by a small, complete set of structural variables:
**Error Intolerance (EI)**: the degree to which ambiguity can be held without forced closure.
**Self-Scaling Capacity (SSC)**: the ability of internal regulation to expand under load.
**Coercive Capacity (CC)**: the ability to externalize regulation into the environment.
**Mortification State**: whether the boundary of solvability has been crossed.

Localization of Regulation: where stability is ultimately restored; internally, externally, or nowhere.

No additional variables are required at this level of abstraction. Differences in biology, history, or circumstance alter load and timing, but not routing.

When discrepancy exceeds tolerance and correction fails, the following outcomes become inevitable:

High error intolerance with insufficient scaling capacity forces reorganization.

Available coercive capacity allows stabilization to be externalized.

Absent coercive capacity requires internal reorganization or collapse.

Absent scaling capacity prevents convergence regardless of tolerance.

After mortification, reorganization is no longer optional.

Psychosis is selected, not expressed.

In dynamical terms, this selection can be understood as a basin transition: once capacity thresholds are crossed, the system settles into the nearest available stable configuration.

The framework permits multiple reorganization outcomes, each restoring stability in a distinct way. These include configurations that stabilize through certainty, through internal reconstruction, through collapse under load, through persistent non-convergence, through external enactment, or through private over-integration.
Only a subset of these arise directly from the envy cycle. Others reflect different constraint geometries that share the same invariant: loss or modification of shared-reality binding.

Psychosis is not, in itself, an index of pathology. Systems with intact, flexible, and reciprocal structures may undergo transient psychotic reorganization when internal capacity is temporarily exceeded. In such cases, reorganization remains internally localized, resolves as solvability returns, and does not require the creation of a bad object or the displacement of regulation into others. Structural severity is therefore not determined by the presence, intensity, or phenomenology of psychosis, but by its function and persistence. Psychosis becomes severe only when it stabilizes by externalizing cost: when coherence is preserved through certainty, coercion, or object degradation rather than recovery. Pathology, in this framework, is defined not by breakdown, but by the necessity of making other systems absorb the cost of one’s stability.

Reorganization stabilizes the system internally, but it does not resolve regulation. Regulation is conserved. What cannot be processed internally must occur elsewhere.

Importantly, the occurrence of psychotic reorganization does not itself indicate pathology. Systems with otherwise intact, flexible, and reciprocal structures may undergo brief, internally contained psychotic states when capacity is temporarily exceeded. In such cases, reorganization is transient, non-instrumental, and resolves as solvability returns.

The sections that follow are therefore not concerned with psychosis per se, but with what happens after reorganization stabilizes in forms that externalize cost. Once a system’s stability depends on displacement rather than recovery, its interactions with other systems are shaped by necessity, not intent. What appears next is not escalation. It is propagation.
