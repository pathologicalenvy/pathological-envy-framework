Chapter Six

# State-Transition Directors Under Constraint

Collapse alone does not determine outcome. What follows collapse is not a free reassembly of structure, nor a random distribution of traits. Reorganization is forced, but it is not unconstrained. The pathways available to a system after mortification are biased by pre-existing regulatory limits that shape which forms of stabilization are energetically viable and which are not.

These limits do not operate as causes of collapse, nor as explanations of personality. They become operative only once regulatory coherence has already failed. Their function is transitional: they bias the direction of reorganization at the moment when a system must stabilize but can no longer do so using prior strategies.

In this sense, they function as state-transition directors - parameters that shape routing without determining outcome, and that influence stabilization without conferring permanence.

### State-Transition Directors

Within the present framework, three directors are sufficient to account for the observed divergence of post-collapse configurations. They are not traits, diagnoses, or identities. They do not describe what a system is, but what it can tolerate, deploy, or coordinate once collapse has occurred.

All three state-transition directors are evaluated simultaneously at the point of reorganization; post-collapse routing reflects their joint feasibility under constraint rather than any sequential ordering or dominance among them.

These directors are treated as capacity constraints with biological and developmental bounds. Their relevance is conditional: they exert no organizing influence prior to mortification and do not initiate collapse. They become decisive only when reorganization is unavoidable.

### Transition Cost and Interface Availability

In some systems, the same alignment of state-transition directors that biases post-collapse stabilization also collapses the energetic cost of switching between already-viable regulatory strategies. Where multiple modes of regulation remain simultaneously sustainable under the same constraint profile, transitions between them do not require reorganization, junctional instability, or renewed collapse.

In such cases, regulatory strategies function as parallel interfaces rather than sequential outcomes. Engagement, withdrawal, dominance, or instrumental pro-sociality may be activated or suspended rapidly in response to environmental affordance - particularly the presence or absence of extractable objects - without loss of coherence or disruption of self-reference.

This capacity reflects transition efficiency, not fragmentation. The system does not reconstitute itself when switching; it selects among pre-stabilized interfaces whose availability is maintained by the same underlying director alignment.

In such systems, post-collapse regulation does not require reorganization at all; stabilization is achieved by selecting among already-viable regulatory interfaces rather than constructing new ones.

Rapid switching between regulatory strategies in such systems may resemble dissociative state changes at a behavioral level. This resemblance is superficial. The transitions described here occur within a single coherent self-system with continuous memory and agency. They do not involve partitioning of identity, encapsulation of affect, or loss of ownership across states. Rather than alternation between discrete selves, these systems exhibit efficient routing among multiple pre-stabilized regulatory interfaces whose availability is maintained by the same underlying director alignment.

Switching, in this sense, functions to preserve solvability rather than to contain fragmentation. Although both dissociative and non-dissociative systems may deploy state transitions to prevent regulatory failure under exposure, they differ in what is permitted to fracture. Dissociative architectures preserve local coherence by allowing global partitioning. The configurations described here preserve global coherence by maintaining transition efficiency. The similarity lies in the use of switching as a protective operation; the difference lies in the structural cost paid to achieve it.

### STD-Relative Cost Topology

Regulatory cost is not absolute. It is determined by the configuration of the state-transition directors themselves. No regulatory strategy is universally lowest-cost; what functions as a failsafe in one system may be energetically prohibitive in another.

This is most clearly observed in the distribution of feedback tolerance. For some configurations, external feedback constitutes a primary source of exposure and destabilization. In such systems, regulation that minimizes or eliminates reliance on feedback may be comparatively inexpensive, even when it appears effortful or rigid at the behavioral level. For other configurations, external feedback is metabolically efficient, while internal containment or unilateral execution rapidly exhausts capacity. In these systems, withdrawal or feedback-independent regulation carries a higher energetic cost than continued interaction, even under strain.

Phenomena commonly described as introversion or extroversion reflect these underlying cost asymmetries rather than stylistic preference or motivational orientation. Introversive expressions correspond to configurations in which internal or feedback-independent regulation remains viable under load. Extroversive expressions correspond to configurations in which external engagement remains the lowest-cost regulatory interface despite increasing exposure.

Accordingly, feedback-independent regulation should not be treated as a universal failsafe or as an inherently simpler solution. Its viability is conditional. It becomes selectively available only where the state-transition directors render interactive feedback disproportionately costly relative to unilateral or internally mediated stabilization. Where this condition is not met, attempts to disengage from feedback increase rather than reduce regulatory load.

This principle clarifies why certain stabilization strategies emerge late, rarely, or only under severe constraint. It also explains why systems differ so markedly in their persistence with interaction, escalation, or engagement even when such strategies appear destabilizing to external observers. What appears irrational or avoidant at the behavioral level may reflect accurate cost minimization within a specific state-transition topology.

### Self-Scaling Capacity

Self-scaling capacity refers to a system’s ability to tolerate asymmetry by up-regulating self-reference under threat without loss of coherence. It governs whether elevation of the self, rather than reduction of exposure, can function as a viable regulatory strategy.

High self-scaling capacity allows stabilization through hierarchy, dominance, or entitlement by enlarging the self as a reference frame. Where this capacity is available, shame-based collapse is less likely to force withdrawal or narrative fixation. Where it is unavailable, attempts at power-based stabilization rapidly destabilize or fail.

Self-scaling capacity does not reflect self-esteem, grandiosity, or confidence. It does not initiate collapse. It determines only whether dominance-based stabilization can be sustained after collapse has already occurred.

Although self-scaling capacity is most readily observed in situations of direct social comparison, it is not limited to interpersonal rivalry. Any condition that renders asymmetry visible under exposure can lens this capacity. Structural status hierarchies, audience asymmetry, dependency on gatekeepers, temporal lag, and implicit normative standards may all exert comparable pressure without requiring a specific comparison target. In such cases, the system is not responding to another person per se, but to the fact of unequal positioning itself. Self-scaling capacity therefore governs tolerance for asymmetry under exposure, regardless of whether that asymmetry is embodied, structural, or ambient.

### Coercive Capacity

Coercive capacity refers to the availability of force (behavioral, social, or structural) as a means of regulating exposure by constraining the environment rather than adapting to it. It includes not only physical aggression, but any reliable capacity to impose limits, enforce outcomes, or shape external conditions.

When coercive capacity is available, power-based stabilization becomes energetically efficient. When it is unavailable, punished, or too costly to deploy, stabilization must occur through withdrawal, narrative restructuring, or internal containment.

Coercive capacity does not imply violence, hostility, or intent. Its presence or absence describes environmental leverage, not motivation. It determines whether the external world can be regulated directly or must instead be avoided, reinterpreted, or disengaged from.

### Error Intolerance

Error intolerance refers to a system’s capacity to sustain ambiguity, unresolved discrepancy, and incomplete coherence without regulatory failure. It governs whether uncertainty can be held provisionally or whether closure must be imposed to restore stability.

High error intolerance biases reorganization toward rigid narrative locks, delusional coherence, or psychotic repair, where ambiguity is eliminated rather than managed. Low error intolerance permits provisional narratives, partial detachment, and flexible stabilization at the cost of increased exposure.

One common manifestation of high error intolerance is narrative inelasticity: resistance to revising explanatory coherence in response to disconfirming external input. Under sufficient load, contradiction does not prompt narrative update but instead triggers narrative hardening, inversion, or dismissal. To external observers - particularly envied objects - this resistance may appear indistinguishable from delusion. Structurally, however, reality testing remains intact; what fails is tolerance for unresolved discrepancy. Narrative inelasticity reflects enforced coherence under constraint rather than primary perceptual disturbance.

Low error intolerance does not imply comfort, emotional ease, or reduced cost; it reflects the capacity to remain exposed to unresolved discrepancy without imposing premature coherence.

Error intolerance is not cognitive rigidity, anxiety sensitivity, or intelligence. It does not generate meaning. It constrains how much unresolved discrepancy can be tolerated before coherence must be enforced.

While the directors above constrain which forms of stabilization are viable at the point of reorganization, they do not alone explain how frequently systems are driven into collapse or the urgency under which reorganization occurs.

### Mortification Prevention Pathways

Systems exposed to destabilizing contradiction do not respond through a single defensive mechanism. Prior to collapse, multiple orthogonal pathways exist through which mortification may be avoided, deferred, or redistributed. These pathways differ in energetic cost, reliability, and structural consequence, and their availability is determined by environmental affordance, regulatory capacity, and exposure constraints rather than preference or intent.

Narrative rigidity is therefore not a default response to threat, but one option among several that is typically deployed only when alternative routes such as spatial extrication, regulatory mode switching, dominance-based containment, or object reassignment are unavailable, ineffective, or themselves mortifying. As exposure becomes unavoidable and collapse proximity increases, lower-cost pathways are exhausted first. Higher-cost mechanisms are engaged only under constraint. 

Understanding narrative inelasticity requires situating it within this broader mortification-prevention space: as a late-stage coherence clamp that preserves continuity when exit, substitution, or detachment can no longer be achieved without destabilization.

Mortification-prevention pathways are therefore not selected deliberatively but activated by elimination: lower-cost routes fail first under constraint, and higher-cost mechanisms engage only when they become the sole remaining means of preserving coherence.

The following section examines this mechanism in detail and distinguishes it from psychotic repair, which represents a categorically different response to unresolvable exposure.

### Narrative Inelasticity and Psychotic Delusion

Certain envy-driven stabilization patterns produce narratives that are exceptionally resistant to external factual correction. Under sufficient load, these narratives may harden to the point that, from the perspective of external observers, they appear indistinguishable from delusion. This resemblance is phenomenological rather than structural. Within the present framework, the mechanisms that produce envy-based narrative inelasticity and those that produce psychotic delusion are distinct and arise under different constraint profiles.

Envy-driven narrative inelasticity reflects enforced coherence under threat while reality testing remains intact. Disconfirming information is perceived, but selectively rejected, reinterpreted, or inverted in domains where narrative revision would destabilize regulatory function. The narrative remains load-bearing: it preserves hierarchy, justifies externalization, or prevents collapse by maintaining a viable explanatory frame. Resistance to correction is therefore conditional and strategic, even when it appears extreme.

Psychotic delusion, by contrast, emerges when reality testing itself fails following catastrophic collapse and no viable stabilization strategy remains available. In such cases, delusional content does not function to defend a position, preserve hierarchy, or regulate external objects. It constitutes emergency coherence construction in the absence of a shared reality. Contradiction does not provoke narrative hardening but fails to register as meaningful signal.

Because these two mechanisms can appear superficially similar while differing fundamentally in structure, function, and reversibility, a direct comparison is provided below. This comparison is not intended to introduce typologies or diagnoses, but to prevent category confusion between envy-based narrative enforcement and psychotic repair.

### Object Scarcity as a Transition-Bias Parameter

While the state-transition directors described above constrain which regulatory strategies are viable at the point of reorganization, they do not alone determine the frequency with which systems are driven into collapse, nor the pressure under which post-collapse routing occurs. A further variable is therefore required to account for differential exposure to mortification and repeated reorganization across otherwise comparable systems. This variable is object scarcity.

Object scarcity refers to the availability of viable external objects capable of absorbing regulatory load without precipitating collapse. It does not describe attachment need, relational desire, or interpersonal preference. Rather, it specifies a structural condition: whether the system can reliably assume the presence of alternative regulatory surfaces when a given object is lost, withdrawn, or rendered unavailable.

Object scarcity is not a state, a director, or an outcome. It functions as a transition-bias parameter that modulates the likelihood, urgency, and energetic cost of reorganization once collapse becomes imminent. Where object scarcity is high, the loss or withdrawal of a single object exerts disproportionate destabilizing pressure. Where object scarcity is low, the same loss may be absorbed with minimal disruption.

Within the present framework, object scarcity can be formalized as a function of four interacting factors:
1.	Devaluation amplitude — the intensity with which regulatory discharge destabilizes or humiliates external objects.
2.	Devaluation calibration — the system’s capacity to titrate devaluation relative to object tolerance, rather than overshooting thresholds that precipitate withdrawal.
3.	Object tolerance — the degree to which external objects can withstand devaluation without disengaging, shaped by their own structural cohesion, boundaries, and alternative supports.
4.	Replacement availability — the density of viable alternative objects within the system’s regulatory ecology.

Variation in object scarcity across otherwise comparable systems reflects not only differences in devaluation amplitude and object tolerance, but also differences in the precision with which devaluation can be titrated relative to object-specific withdrawal thresholds. This precision, here termed devaluation calibration capacity, describes a system’s ability to apply negative regulatory pressure without precipitating disengagement. Where this capacity is low, devaluation overshoots withdrawal thresholds, accelerating object loss and scarcity. Where it is high, regulatory pressure may be sustained within tolerable limits, preserving object availability under continued envy activation.

Taken together, these factors determine the effective size and stability of the system’s object pool. Chronic object scarcity emerges not simply from the loss of objects, but from a persistent mismatch between devaluation amplitude and object tolerance, compounded by limited replacement availability. Systems that repeatedly overshoot devaluation thresholds or operate within sparse object ecologies experience accelerated object loss, shortened object half-life, and recurrent scarcity phases.

Object scarcity exerts its influence indirectly. It does not initiate collapse, nor does it select specific stabilization strategies. Instead, it biases transition dynamics by increasing the probability that collapse will occur under conditions of high urgency and limited affordance. High object scarcity increases the likelihood that mortification will be experienced as catastrophic rather than absorbable, and that post-collapse reorganization will proceed under severe constraint. Conversely, low object scarcity buffers systems against repeated collapse by distributing regulatory load across multiple viable surfaces.

Importantly, object scarcity is not reducible to any of the state-transition directors described above. Rather, it interacts with them. Scarcity amplifies the effects of low coercive capacity, low self-scaling capacity, or high error intolerance by narrowing the range of energetically viable stabilization options. Where scarcity is extreme, even otherwise tolerable director alignments may fail to converge, precipitating oscillation, emergency repair, or repeated collapse.

Accordingly, object scarcity is treated here as a derived transition-bias parameter: one that conditions how often systems are forced into reorganization and how constrained post-collapse routing becomes, without introducing new states or altering the fundamental structure of the transition space.

### Constraint, Not Determinism

Questions of durability, persistence, and irreversibility are bracketed here; the present chapter addresses only the availability and energetic viability of stabilization strategies at the point of reorganization, not their long-term consolidation.

These directors do not function independently, nor do they operate as fixed determinants. They bias transitions by shaping which stabilization strategies remain viable under given conditions. The same mortification may yield different configurations across systems not because collapse differed, but because post-collapse affordances did.

State-transition directors constrain routing. They do not fix endpoints.

Stabilization configurations are therefore not treated as permanent end-states, but as locally stable regulatory solutions whose persistence depends on environmental affordances, energetic cost, and exposure tolerance.

### Integration and Non-Convergence

Where the directors can be synchronized at the system level, reorganization converges on a locally dominant stabilization solution. Where they impose incompatible requirements—such that no single strategy can satisfy tolerance, leverage, and coherence simultaneously—convergence becomes unstable or fails entirely.

In such cases, oscillation, fragmentation, or emergency repair may occur.

The directors remain operative even in failure. Non-convergence does not imply their absence, but their inability to synchronize into a globally viable solution.

### Boundary Conditions

Nothing in this account implies inevitability, moral judgment, or permanence. The directors described here bias transition under constraint; they do not explain origin, justify outcome, or exhaust the complexity of lived experience.

Their role is limited and precise: to explain why, once collapse has occurred, reorganization does not proceed arbitrarily—and why the same structural failure can yield divergent forms of stabilization across systems. Accordingly, this chapter specifies the constraints that bias post-collapse routing; questions of persistence, ecological propagation, and irreversibility are addressed in the analysis that follows.

### Applied Configuration: Maternal Expression Under Constraint

The mechanisms described above admit a range of ecological configurations in which asymmetry, dependency, and replacement constraints alter how regulatory pressure is expressed without altering the underlying structure of the cycle. One such configuration—particularly illustrative of object scarcity under constraint—is the maternal expression of the pathological envy cycle.

This configuration does not constitute a distinct diagnosis, trait, or personality category. It represents a role-bounded manifestation of the same regulatory dynamics operating under conditions of prolonged object non-replaceability, high dependency asymmetry, and constrained exit. The maternal role intensifies the salience of these conditions without introducing new mechanisms.

### Ecological Constraints

In maternal configurations, the envied object is structurally non-substitutable over extended developmental periods. Replacement availability is therefore minimal by definition, and object scarcity is chronically elevated even in the absence of overt conflict. The maternal system cannot reliably assume alternative regulatory surfaces, nor can it disengage without cost. As a result, regulatory pressure concentrates on the single available object.

Additionally, maternal configurations often lack access to overt coercive strategies without social or internal penalty. Direct aggression, explicit dominance, or open devaluation risks immediate relational rupture or external sanction. Consequently, regulatory strategies are forced to operate through deniable channels that preserve apparent care while constraining autonomy.

### Regulatory Strategy Under Scarcity

Under these constraints, regulation shifts from overt behavioral control toward temporal and predictive control. Rather than correcting actions after they occur, the system attempts to occupy the object’s future in advance. Anticipatory questioning, pattern prediction, and verification behaviors serve to reduce uncertainty, pre-empt deviation, and neutralize surprise.

This temporal capture does not function to guide behavior toward specific outcomes. Its function is regulatory: by collapsing the object’s future into predictability, the system reduces exposure to envy-evoking discrepancy and mitigates fragmentation anxiety under scarcity. Where prediction succeeds, autonomy is compressed without confrontation. Where prediction fails, devaluation or withdrawal may follow as secondary regulation.

Importantly, these behaviors often masquerade as concern, planning, or nurturance. This camouflage is not incidental. It reflects the necessity of maintaining relational viability while exerting control in an environment where overt domination is energetically or socially costly.

### Interaction With State-Transition Directors

Maternal configurations amplify the effects of object scarcity on post-collapse routing. Low replacement availability increases the urgency with which collapse must be avoided or contained through emergency repair, while limited coercive capacity restricts available stabilization strategies. Where self-scaling capacity is also constrained, escalation through dominance becomes unsustainable, biasing the system toward containment through prediction, monitoring, and narrative minimization of autonomy.

High error intolerance further intensifies these dynamics. Uncertainty regarding the object’s independent activity becomes difficult to tolerate, increasing reliance on anticipatory control. In such cases, repeated minor deviations may accumulate sufficient pressure to precipitate mortification, particularly if object reconstitution fails or autonomy is interpreted as rejection.

### Severity Gradient and Escalation Risk

Within this configuration, escalation occurs not through immediate annihilation but through progressive autonomy incapacitation. Early expressions may involve benign-appearing curiosity or interest in routines. Under sustained scarcity or failed regulation, these behaviors may intensify into preemptive scripting, identity compression, or persistent monitoring. The risk is not sudden collapse, but gradual erosion of object autonomy that increases the probability of later destabilization.

The maternal configuration thus demonstrates how object scarcity can drive high-frequency regulatory engagement without altering the structure of the envy cycle itself. The same transition-bias parameters apply; only the ecological constraints differ.

### Scope and Limits

This account does not imply intent, moral culpability, or inevitability. It specifies a structural pathway by which pathological envy may be expressed under particular constraints. Many maternal systems do not exhibit these dynamics, and the presence of the maternal role alone is neither sufficient nor necessary for their emergence.

The purpose of this section is illustrative. By examining a configuration in which object scarcity is chronic and exit is constrained, the framework’s explanatory capacity is stress-tested. The mechanisms described here derive directly from the general model and require no additional assumptions.
