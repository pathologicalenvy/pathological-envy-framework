Chapter 3 — The Orphan Problem of Psychosis

3.1 Overview

This chapter explains why psychosis lacks a unified theoretical owner. Despite extensive study across psychiatry, psychoanalysis, psychology, and neuroscience, psychosis remains conceptually fragmented. Each discipline describes aspects of psychosis accurately, yet none specifies the process by which psychosis emerges as a failure state.

This absence of ownership reflects not neglect, but a missing level of analysis: formal failure logic.

3.2 Fragmentation Across Disciplines

Psychosis is addressed differently across fields:
-	Psychiatry emphasizes symptom clusters, diagnosis, and pharmacologic response.
-	Psychoanalysis emphasizes meaning, symbolization, and breakdown of containment.
-	Cognitive models emphasize belief formation, attribution, and inference errors.
-	Neuroscience emphasizes circuit dysfunction, salience misassignment, and prediction error.

Each approach captures part of the phenomenon at different levels of analysis. None specifies how these elements converge into a single failure condition.

3.3 Description Without Process

Most accounts of psychosis are descriptive rather than procedural. They catalog features (hallucinations, delusions, disorganization) but do not specify state transitions.

As a result, psychosis is often treated as an accumulation of symptoms rather than as a transition into a different mode of organization. This obscures why onset may be sudden, why course is variable, and why stabilization may occur without elimination of vulnerability.

3.4 The Absence of Failure Conditions

A defining limitation of existing models is the absence of explicit failure conditions. Psychosis is rarely defined in terms of what has failed.

Without failure conditions, psychosis appears mysterious or irreducible. It is attributed to excesses (too much dopamine, too much meaning, too much stress) rather than to loss of function at the regulatory level.

This excess framing obscures the possibility that psychosis reflects insufficient regulation, not excessive activity.

3.5 Competing Ownership Claims

Each discipline implicitly claims partial ownership of psychosis:
-	Biological models claim causality through mechanism.
-	Interpretive models claim meaning through narrative.
-	Cognitive models claim explanation through inference.

Because none specifies collapse conditions, none can fully claim ownership. Psychosis remains theoretically orphaned, passed between models without integration.

3.6 The Need for a Process-Level Model

Psychosis requires a process-level explanation that specifies:
-	what variable is being regulated,
-	how regulation normally occurs,
-	how escalation unfolds,
-	what collapse means,
-	and what outcomes follow collapse.

Without this structure, psychosis cannot be integrated with models of affect regulation, relational dynamics, or learning failure.

3.7 Psychosis as Outcome, Not Origin

In this framework, psychosis is not treated as a primary disorder. It is treated as an outcome: a terminal state reached when regulation fails within a given cycle.

This reframing allows psychosis to be understood without reducing it to envy, trauma, cognition, or neurochemistry alone. These factors influence thresholds and pathways, but the failure logic remains the same.

3.8 Why Psychosis Appears Discontinuous

Psychosis often appears to emerge abruptly. This apparent discontinuity reflects the fact that collapse is a threshold event, not a gradual accumulation.

Without a model of escalation and collapse, onset appears inexplicable. With such a model, suddenness becomes expected.

3.9 Ethical Consequences of Orphaning

Theoretical orphaning has ethical consequences. When psychosis lacks ownership, responsibility is displaced. Individuals are blamed, pathologized, or mystified. Interventions become reactive rather than anticipatory.

A failure-based model restores accountability to systems rather than to character.

3.10 Summary

Psychosis remains theoretically orphaned because existing models describe its features without specifying the failure conditions that produce it. Without formal collapse logic, psychosis is framed as excess rather than loss of regulation capacity.

This manuscript proposes that psychosis is best understood as an outcome of regulatory failure. The following chapters specify the regulatory cycle in detail, beginning with its architecture and operational states.
