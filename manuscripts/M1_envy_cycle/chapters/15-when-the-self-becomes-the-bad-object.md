Chapter 15 — When the Self Becomes the Bad Object

15.1 Overview

This chapter specifies the subjective configuration that emerges following regulatory failure in the pathological envy cycle. When psychosis occurs, the system no longer succeeds in externalizing shame, fixing it onto an object, or disengaging from exposure. As a result, the self becomes the primary site of regulation.

This transformation represents a qualitative shift in organization. Shame, previously managed through relational or environmental mechanisms, is now experienced directly and without mediation.

15.2 Failure of Projection

Under ordinary operation of the envy cycle, shame is externalized through projection onto an object. Devaluation, grievance, idealization, and withdrawal all depend on the capacity to locate threat or deficiency outside the self.

In psychosis, this capacity fails. Projection no longer relocates shame effectively. Attempts to attribute threat or inferiority to an external object do not stabilize the system and therefore cannot contain exposure.

The failure of projection leaves shame unassigned within the regulatory architecture. Projection fails here not because the external object is unavailable, but because externalization no longer produces regulation.

15.3 Collapse of the Self–Object Boundary

As projection fails, the boundary between self and object deteriorates. Distinctions that previously organized experience (inside versus outside, self versus other, source versus target) lose coherence.

This collapse does not imply confusion in the ordinary sense. Rather, it reflects the system’s inability to maintain separations that previously allowed shame to be managed at a distance.

When the self–object boundary collapses, the self becomes vulnerable to direct exposure.

15.4 Unmediated Shame

The defining feature of this state is unmediated shame. Shame is no longer transformed, displaced, or buffered by regulatory processes. It intrudes directly into conscious experience.

This intrusion is not episodic. It saturates perception, interpretation, and meaning. Ordinary events are experienced as exposing, accusatory, or revealing because the system lacks mechanisms for shielding the self from evaluative threat.

15.5 The Self as the Bad Object

With externalization unavailable, the self assumes the role previously occupied by the envied or devalued object. The self is experienced as defective, dangerous, contaminated, or fundamentally compromised.

This configuration does not require explicit self-criticism. The experience may manifest as persecution, reference, or threat, but the organizing structure remains the same: the self is positioned as the locus of badness.

The term ‘bad object’ here refers to regulatory positioning rather than moral evaluation or conscious self-judgment.

15.6 Emergence of Persecutory Experience

Persecutory experience arises when unmediated shame is interpreted through residual attempts at meaning-making. External agents may be perceived as hostile, judging, or controlling, but these perceptions are secondary to the underlying exposure.

Persecution does not eliminate shame. It expresses it in distorted form, including delusional attribution when direct attribution to the self becomes intolerable.

15.7 Loss of Regulatory Distance

A critical feature of this state is the loss of regulatory distance. There is no longer sufficient separation between experience and evaluation to allow modulation.

Thoughts, perceptions, and sensations acquire immediate personal significance. This immediacy reflects the absence of buffering mechanisms rather than heightened sensitivity.

15.8 Instability of Meaning

Meaning becomes unstable because it is no longer constrained by feedback-dependent regulation. Interpretations proliferate or fragment in response to uncontained shame.

This instability does not reflect cognitive deterioration. It reflects the absence of a regulatory framework capable of filtering significance.

Logical capacity may remain intact even as interpretive filtering fails.

15.9 Summary

When the self becomes the bad object, psychosis assumes its characteristic subjective structure. Projection fails, the self–object boundary collapses, and shame intrudes without mediation.

Persecutory experience, referential meaning, and fragmentation arise as secondary attempts to manage exposure when regulation has failed. The following chapter examines how some psychotic states stabilize through delusional repair, while others remain acute and transient.
