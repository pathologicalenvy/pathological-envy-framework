Chapter 18 — Many Cycles, One Failure Pattern

18.1 Overview

This chapter generalizes the regulatory failure logic developed in the preceding chapters beyond pathological envy while preserving specificity. The aim is not to universalize envy as the cause of all dysregulation, but to demonstrate that distinct regulatory cycles can share common failure dynamics.

Different systems regulate different variables. What unifies them is not content, but the structure of escalation, feedback dependence, and collapse.

18.2 Envy as One Regulatory Cycle

The pathological envy cycle regulates shame and exposure through externalization, devaluation, and feedback. Its defining features include reliance on relational impact, negative reinforcement, and escalation under uncertainty.

This cycle represents one instantiation of a broader class of regulatory systems in which relief depends on interaction with the environment rather than on internal processing.

18.3 Other Regulatory Cycles

Other affective and cognitive systems operate through analogous cycles, despite regulating different variables. Examples include:
•	Mood regulation, which attempts to stabilize affect through activity, withdrawal, or interpretation
•	Trauma regulation, which attempts to control threat through avoidance, hypervigilance, or reenactment
•	Salience regulation, which governs assignment of significance and relevance
•	Prediction regulation, which attempts to minimize surprise and uncertainty

Each of these systems operates through actions intended to reduce an aversive internal state by altering external or internal conditions.

18.4 Shared Dependence on Feedback

Across regulatory cycles, relief depends on feedback confirming efficacy. Whether the feedback is emotional, behavioral, sensory, or symbolic, it serves the same function: confirmation that regulation has succeeded.

When feedback fails or becomes uninterpretable, escalation occurs. This escalation reflects uncertainty rather than excess and persists as long as predictive solvability remains.

18.5 Common Escalation Dynamics

Despite differences in regulated variables, escalation dynamics share key features:
•	Iterative trial-and-error
•	Adjustment of intensity, scope, or strategy
•	Persistence despite stable affect
•	Increasing rigidity as uncertainty grows

These features indicate that escalation is a structural response to failed relief, not a domain-specific phenomenon.

18.6 Convergent Collapse Conditions

Collapse occurs across cycles when predictive solvability fails. This failure is marked by loss of confidence that any available action will reduce the regulated variable.

At this point, the system must exit active regulation and transition to stabilization or failure. The specific form of collapse may vary, but the underlying condition is the same: ERP approaches zero.

18.7 Divergence of Outcomes

While collapse conditions converge, outcomes diverge based on system-specific variables, environmental constraints, and available stabilization pathways.
Some systems stabilize through narrative certainty, others through withdrawal, suppression, or reorganization of meaning. Psychosis represents one possible outcome when no stabilization pathway remains viable.

18.8 Limits of Generalization

This framework does not assert that all dysregulation reduces to envy or that all psychosis originates from a single cycle. It specifies a shared failure pattern, not a universal cause.

Biological, developmental, and contextual factors influence which cycles are active, how they escalate, and which stabilizations are available.

18.9 Implications for Theory Integration

Recognizing shared failure patterns allows integration across theoretical domains without reduction. Psychoanalytic, cognitive, and neurobiological accounts can be understood as describing different levels of the same regulatory processes.

This integration preserves domain-specific insights while clarifying how disparate phenomena converge under failure.

18.10 Summary

Different regulatory cycles govern different variables, but they share common escalation and collapse dynamics. Failure occurs when feedback-dependent regulation loses solvability and no fallback remains.

Understanding these shared patterns allows psychosis and other extreme outcomes to be conceptualized as predictable failures of regulation rather than isolated anomalies. The next chapter states the unifying claim of the book explicitly and delineates its scope and limits.
